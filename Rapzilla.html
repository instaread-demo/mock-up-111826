Do White People Listen to Christian Rap? Caleb Gordon’s Comments Vs Industry Data

By Justin Sarachik
November 18, 2026
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 10:11
Instaread

Caleb Gordon sparked another debate. This time it was on the You Can’t Say That In Church (Say Dat Pod), claiming white people (Christians) don’t listen to CHH. Here’s some data we found to question that.

Derek Minor and Parris Chariz host the popular faith-and-culture podcast. Gordon sat down with them for Episode 40, titled “Is Caleb Gordon Too Controversial?”

Caleb Gordon made a blunt claim about the racial demographics of Christian music listeners during the conversation.

“White people don’t listen to Christian rap,” Gordon said on the podcast. “They listen to Brandon Lake.”

He went even further when describing the crowds at his own live concerts.

“The white people come to my show because, thank God, Forrest [Frank] put me on that song,” Gordon added. “And that’s the only song they know from me.”

Gordon believes this from experience. He noted: “If you ask a crowd at one of these shows, ‘Who has ever been to a Christian rap concert?’ almost no hands go up. But if you mention worship, everybody knows.”

He further explained that his live supporters are largely new converts transitioning straight out of mainstream secular hip-hop or are unchurched. “The people coming up to me after these shows aren’t traditional church kids. They’re guys who just got out of prison, or they’re brand new believers transitioning straight out of listening to Drake…”

His comments immediately went crazy across Instagram. The White CHH fans showed up!

The Comments

User @dunningbaborons wrote, “Imagine taking all the white listeners away from Derek Minor and Lecrae and KB and Trip and Mineo etc…. Hate to say it, but Christian rappers would be in TROUBLE. I would venture to say most listeners of Christian rap are white.”

Another user, @thisishonore, echoed that sentiment bluntly: “Not trueee bro! More like 70% of CHH is suburban YT kids.”

Other fans jumped in to point out the flawed logic of writing off an entire demographic. User @caleb_j_music commented, “Bro has clearly never been to a Winter Jam or a Lecrae concert in the suburbs. White kids make up a massive chunk of this culture.”

That comment, however, is a little complicated. It would be safe to say the majority of the Winter Jam crowd is not there for CHH, but at the same time, rappers could gain many fans and bring them over from there.

User @hiphopapologist added fuel to the fire, writing, “This is a wild take considering how many white youth groups and suburban kids sustain indie CHH labels. Brandon Lake is huge, but acting like the crossover doesn’t exist is crazy.”

Rapzilla’s Chad Horton chimed in on the thread with hard data, commenting: “What? Most of CHH listeners are white. 60% are white. 40% identify with a ‘minority ethnicity.’ I’ve been in for 25 years and in the data. The facts.”

As user @kingdombeats_ summarized the general consensus of the comment section: “He’s letting his personal tour experience with Forrest Frank cloud the actual numbers. White people definitely listen to Christian rap—they just listen to the ones on major playlists and crossover tracks.”

Gordon’s literal statement is technically false, but let’s look at what he actually said.

The Streaming Gap Between Worship, Crossover Pop, and Core CHH

Streaming numbers prove that mainstream Contemporary Christian Music (CCM) and worship operate on a completely different commercial tier than core Christian hip-hop (CHH).

Luminate’s 2025 Year-End Report revealed that Christian and gospel music was the fastest-growing genre category in the United States. On-demand audio volume for the category surged 18.5% year-over-year, vastly outpacing the 4.6% growth across the broader U.S. music market.

Brandon Lake and Forrest Frank propelled that historic surge. Both artists placed songs simultaneously on the Billboard Hot 100 for the first time since 2014, according to the Associated Press.

Forrest Frank commands roughly 6.9 million monthly listeners on Spotify alone. His solo hits like “GOOD DAY” and “YOUR WAY’S BETTER” have each surpassed 200 million streams on the platform.

Core Christian hip-hop artists pull impressive numbers, yet they remain well behind those crossover totals. *NF was part of this chart.

Forrest and Caleb

Gordon’s personal Spotify catalog directly validates his point about the “Forrest Frank effect” drawing wider notice, even if his blanket statement about white listeners missed the mark.

Frank tapped Gordon for the 2024 single “GOD IS GOOD.” That collaboration has racked up more than 84.7 million streams on Spotify as of November 2026.

Viberate streaming analytics show that “GOD IS GOOD” still generates nearly 380,000 weekly streams for Gordon. His biggest solo tracks, such as “War” and “Fruits of My Labor,” pull between 48,000 and 126,000 weekly streams by comparison.

Forrest Frank’s third-largest traffic driver for Gordon on Spotify is the official “This Is Forrest Frank” editorial playlist. Even on tour, Air1 reported that Frank brought a fan on stage every night during his Jesus Generation Tour to rap Gordon’s verse on “GOD IS GOOD.”

So while it’s correct that Frank’s predominantly white audience is bringing in his streams, that doesn’t mean that white people aren’t making up a majority of his audience if you take Frank’s streams away.

Breaking Down the Listener Demographics

No public Nielsen or Luminate report isolates the exact racial breakdown of standalone Christian hip-hop listeners. Industry trackers lump CHH into either the broader Christian/Gospel bucket or the mainstream R&B/Hip-Hop category. A problem for another day.

Available data and overwhelming audience feedback still paint a pretty clear picture of who consumes these sounds.

NPR and Luminate Insights data show that the booming Christian and gospel streaming audience is 60% female and 30% Millennial. Historically, traditional CCM and worship formats draw a predominantly white evangelical audience, often estimated at over 70% in religious market research.

Christian hip-hop outlets reflect a far more diverse split than Gordon suggested.

BOOST Radio operates the largest multi-market Christian hip-hop and rhythmic CHR radio network in the United States. The network’s official media kit reports that its audience is 48% White, while Black, Hispanic, and other listeners make up the remaining 52%.

Global digital metrics skew even less white and less American.

YouTube demographic estimates from Nexlev for Reach Records show that only 26.6% of its viewership comes from the United States. Nearly 70% of Reach Records’ YouTube audience is male, reflecting heavy consumption across Latin America, Africa, and Europe.

Spanish-language Christian rap also represents one of the genre’s biggest demographics. Veterans like Redimi2 and Funky consistently pull between 1.6 million and 2.4 million monthly Spotify listeners from predominantly Hispanic audiences.

Hard Ticket Sales

Live box-office numbers expose the sharpest contrast between CCM-adjacent crossover acts and traditional Christian rappers.

Pollstar’s Q3 2026 box-office charts ranked Forrest Frank as the top touring act in Christian and gospel music. Frank grossed $49.02 million across 565,207 tickets sold between November 2025 and August 2026.

Pollstar’s LIVE75 chart also placed Frank at No. 5 globally in mid-2026, making him the only Christian artist in the worldwide top five. His Jesus Generation Tour averaged 18,175 tickets sold and $1.46 million in gross revenue per night.

Forrest Frank moved 27,365 hard tickets at Hersheypark Stadium in Pennsylvania and 23,667 tickets at GEODIS Park in Nashville.

NF, who has a large Christian audience, commands similar arena-level demand from a heavily white fanbase. Pollstar reports from his recent arena run show an average of 8,971 tickets sold per show at $76.54 per ticket, grossing $686,718 nightly.

Core Christian hip-hop headliners operate on a much smaller scale.

Solo theater and club runs for Lecrae have historically averaged between 1,300 and 1,500 tickets per night, according to Pollstar Concert Pulse archives. CHH artists see their strongest ticket velocity in diverse spots like Atlanta, Dallas, Houston, Chicago, and New York City.

Traditional Christian rappers typically only reach 10,000-seat arenas when booked on multi-artist evangelical package tours.

Annual arena tours like Winter Jam have featured Lecrae, Andy Mineo, KB, and Hulvey alongside worship headliners. Winter Jam draws 10,000 to 13,000 attendees per night, moving over 500,000 tickets annually to predominantly white church groups. Again, it’s a perfect place to gain a new demographic of fans. It would be safe to assume many of those fans have crossed over into the artists’ fanbase. It also coincides with the popularity of an artist like Hulvey.

The Verdict on Caleb Gordon’s “white people” Take

But perhaps these more popular artists have discovered the secret sauce. By tapping into their majority white audience and taking influence from the worship artists that are popular, they can make music more digestible for a wider variety. KB has toured with Brandon Lake and makes worshipful music. Hulvey’s music blurs the lines of rap, pop, and worship much like Frank does.

Gordon might be reaching fewer white audiences because his music is often emulating or reflecting mainstream hip-hop, which is mostly black. However, it would be safe to say that plenty of white people listen to mainstream hip-hop, but those most likely are not the same people listening to Brandon Lake. To reiterate, his opinion is based on his own experience. The overall analysis would say it’s wrong, but not necessarily to him. Derek Minor responded to a few comments in the thread, indicating that he knew what’s up because he’s been around long enough to know the date and see for himself. He also came from the era of youth group touring and Reach Records blowing up.

The overwhelming consensus echoed by hundreds of fans and pros within CHH in the comment sections is that Caleb Gordon delivered an inaccurate soundbite. Although he had his reasons.

As commenter @radicalonfire noted after watching the full exchange, “Gordon said something wild within the flow of a greater conversation, but he was definitely wrong about this.”

White listeners absolutely consume Christian hip-hop. Millions of white fans stream the diverse array of artists in the genre. They are buying tickets and merch too. It doesn’t hurt that many of the artists in CHH are also white, and they were once/still are fans. And while a statement like “white people don’t listen to Christian rap” can be partially true, it could also be unequivocally false.
