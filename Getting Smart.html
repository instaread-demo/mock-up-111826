What We Talk About When We Talk About Agency

Open LinkedIn. Scroll for three minutes, and I bet you’ll find a post like “it’s never been easier to build something” or “AI can do tasks, so we need people with agency to…” 

These proposed silver bullets make a few assumptions: 

  1. It assumes that LinkedIn is representative of the whole labor force while in reality it only represents about 30% of employees and employers and is heavily weighted toward white collar/knowledge work. 
  2. It misses the point that in most of the United States it has actually never been harder to build something in the physical world, which has been discussed at length within the Abundance movement. This bottleneck is due to bureaucracies, red-tape, celebrating the outcome of funding rather than delivering on promises and much more. 

So, who then can act agentically … and should they? In the digital world, it is easier than ever to build things and harder than ever to get the attention of enough users to make it scale and sustain.  So what are these posts actually suggesting? And what is this version of agency that they are describing? 

Agency As Control

While agency is critical to nurture, it is a false promise to say that agency will enable you to survive in the 21st century. In fact, much of the buzzword-y discourse around agency makes it into a trojan horse for “pull yourself up by your bootstraps.” It’s a way to make the current disordered (at best) and dystopic (at worst) economic moment the unique problem of every individual. It’s yet another example of selling both the virus and the cure—and the irony is that it’s coming right from the mouth of those building products that they claim to be agentic. 

In her piece, The Ozempicization of the Economy, economist Kyla Scanlon describes this moment: “The people selling ‘agency’ benefit from a world where nobody trusts institutions, because distrust is the market condition that makes their product necessary.” Her piece goes on to explore the myriad ways that “agency” has become a buzzword used to brand the desperate pursuit of control in an unstable world, citing folks like  Sam Kriss, who describes the “highly agentic” as people who “just do things.” Kriss notes: “An AI might be able to code faster than you, but there is one advantage that humans still have. It’s called agency.” This is the philosophical embodiment of the classic Facebook/Meta adage “move fast and break things” which left ruptures in society that we, and especially our youth, are still clamoring out of. 

Scanlon notes that this is a trend in sectors and habits like betting, body hacking and over optimization in all aspects of life. “What’s being sold as personal control is not actually control… What’s being sold instead is the feeling of individual control through the bet, the hack, the feed, the subscription, the optimization.” What’s being sold is a false definition of agency and, left unchecked, a dangerous one. 

Modern Definitions of Agency

Agency is a neutral word. Like “impact” it can be positive or negative depending on how you use it. For example, you want the electrician that comes to your house to be agentic in the sense that they solve a complex problem with routing a wire, but not necessarily by circumventing code, and cutting corners, and probably not in a way that impacts your neighbor negatively.

Because agency is neutral, it is simultaneously many things and no thing.  According to modern day frameworks and the evangelists in Silicon Valley agency is a skill and disposition. It sets apart the rule followers from the leaders, the workers exposed to AI against the workers that know how to leverage AI. But this is only a partial portrait of the agency.  In fact, there are many modern definitions of agency that help us understand what agency really means in a learning and human development context. As we’ve discussed in previous blogs and podcasts on Getting Smart, Sangeet Paul Choudary has a hypothesis that curiosity -> knowledge -> curation -> action. All but knowledge require agency, and in our modern moment knowledge is “cheaper” than ever. Through this lens,  agency might be the ability to be curious (as opposed to someone else taking your attention), the ability to curate (as opposed to someone else choosing for you), and action ( deciding what to do rather than someone telling you to do something). In Martin Seligman’s new book on agency, it is defined as: 

  • Efficacy: The confidence and belief that you can accomplish a specific task or goal right now, which drives you to try.
  • Optimism: The expectation and belief that your efforts will pay off over the long run, which drives persistence.
  • Imagination: The breadth and ability to envision possibilities and goals that do not yet exist, which drives innovation.

What these definitions start to gesture at is that acting alone is not enough. There is a layer of discernment, judgement and curation required to get there and in a world of exponential generative capability it becomes more important than ever to make the right decisions with how and when to act. Which of the 8 reports that Claude wrote me should I read deeply? Which should I share? Which should I get behind? Is it worth building a company that extracts from the Earth in a harmful manner? Is it worth building a product that is likely to create mental health challenges for young people?

To discern well requires a level of curiosity, curation and an awareness of values and goals which are overlooked building blocks of agentic individuals. Without these, agency is a slippery slope to individualism. It is important to encourage agency as an outcome of our future learners, but it is equally important to not focus so holistically on this bootstraps version of those who can and those who can’t. In our modern discourse, the rise in agency feels similar to that of “grit” in the 2010s, which quickly received a fair amount of criticism for “blaming the victim”. In a 2015 article for EdWeek, Harvard Professor Jal Mehta shared that “a focus on grit is taking a heavily impoverished view of human motivation; in the long run, most people do not persevere at things because they are good at persevering, they persevere because they find things that are worth investing in.”

The answer to this is further investment in purpose and community. It is a more collective skills framework, one that makes a value judgment about not only what a person can do, but how a person can/should be. What this means for education leaders and folks building skills frameworks, is that if agency is one of the desired core outcomes of your system (PoG or otherwise), it needs to be complemented with “citizen”, “member of the community”, and “changemaker”.  Developing and recognizing agency matters but deploying agency matters more and if the role is developing people, that part is critical. 

At RedBridge school in San Francisco, students K-8 work on skills like project management and autonomy, but they also work on things like noticing, building empathy and embedding in community. By starting young, agency and autonomy become a developmentally appropriate progression that helps expand an individual’s locus of influence and accountability as they grow up and get more and more agentic experience.

At Watershed School in Colorado and the Liberty Public School’s EDGE Microschool, the curriculum is explicitly oriented toward the 25 global goals framework referenced in Difference Making at the Heart of Learning.

At NACA Inspired Schools Network (NISN), schools are explicitly rooted in Indigenous community identity — the whole model is premised on the idea that student agency and community sovereignty are inseparable. 

At Green School of Bali, grade 8 students are paired with adult mentors to develop passion projects tied to the UN Sustainable Development Goals—but the whole frame is outward-facing. Students aren’t exploring interests for personal fulfillment; they’re solving problems for their communities. One student designed “electro stairs” to capture energy from footsteps on campus. The agency is real, but it’s always pointed at something beyond the self.

Similarly, a number of frameworks and progressions have been developed to help chart agency. In 2016, Kathleen McClaskey and Barbara Bray put forward a progression of agency, alongside a helpful crosswalk to know it when you see it. Similarly, New Tech Network has a rubric of agency. Fullscale (formerly Aurora Institute) put forward an Agency by Design playbook and Transcend has an Ignity Agency Series. At a broader level, the Inner Development Goals are a great example of a skills framework that foreground relationship, community and collective wellbeing, while also developing the individual skills that can help a person thrive. 

Too often we penalize individuals for not having enough agency, saying they have a skill deficit, but agency is, in many instances, a privilege and one that is enabled or limited by the rules of society and systems. While Seligman’s efficacy, optimism and judgement or Choundry’s curiosity, knowledge, curation, action, can be cultivated in classrooms and through projects as indicated above, it is often catalyzed by economic situations. Without resources, you face more friction when trying to make a healthy decision at a grocery store. Without health care, agency is stymied, as losing health care prevents 24% of employees from leaving jobs they don’t like. Without free speech, it is possible to be the most literate country in the world and still unable to exercise agency. 

If individual agency were the true secret sauce of our economic future, more people would get jobs based on merit and capability, instead more than half of individuals get jobs because they know someone, which has been well documented in the work on social capital.  While expanding a network is sometimes a byproduct of agency (networking, etc.), it is often much more passive and inherited through social status and class.

We are all bearing witness to unprecedented investment in “agentic” technologies at the same time as we see a more quiet divestment from social technologies: third spaces, modes of transportation, intergenerational housing, the kinds of technologies and occasions that create the rich soil for pro-flourishing agency. This is the canary in the coal mine warning of a future sidelining of collective skills and dispositions. In this environment, agency will be a signal of productivity but not of contribution, of building but not creating and it will be reserved for only a select few where the conditions and capacity for agency align. 

In Conclusion

As Scanlon says in her piece that equates agency to ozempic: ““To be clear, Ozempic is a wonderful technology that solves a very real problem for individuals but it leaves the collective problem like the food system and healthcare access untouched.””

To create a thriving society, we cannot rely on billions of agentic individuals making decisions focused on the self over and over again. We must shift our thinking from agency being solely a skill that we develop, to recognizing there are a set of enabling conditions along with a set of enabling capacities. Recognizing this means shifting agency from a privilege for some to a right for all. 

As agentic AI hovers somewhere between hype and revelation, we must remember that agentic AI is fundamentally a servant of one, the user. When this is true it becomes more important than ever to nurture and develop collective agency in our public. Acting along a set of values in service of a collective is a uniquely human trait and one that we must nurture and hone in order to thrive.   

So, after all that, perhaps agency is directional action, in service of a pro-flourishing, pro-human future. That I can get behind.
