Types of coffee means four different things, depending on who is asking. It can be the plant the beans came from, how dark they were roasted, how the coffee was brewed, or the drink in the cup. Each one changes what you taste, so I’ve taken them in that order, from the bean through roast, grind and brew to the drink.

Drinks then split into hot and cold, and after that into how much milk goes in, how much water, and what else gets added.

The four coffee beans

Almost all coffee comes from four kinds of coffee plant: Arabica, Robusta, Liberica and Excelsa. Every coffee you buy is a variety of one of them, or a blend. Arabica and Robusta together account for well over 95% of world production, so those two are what you’ll meet on the shelf.

1. Arabica

Arabica (Coffea arabica) is the most widely grown coffee, at a little under 60% of the world crop. It does best at altitude, in volcanic soil with steady rain, which is why Ethiopia, Colombia, Costa Rica and the highlands of Central America are known for it. The downside is that the plant catches disease easily. The beans are oval and larger than Robusta.

There are dozens of Arabica varieties, including Typica, Bourbon, Caturra, Pacamara and Geisha. Each tastes a little different, but as a group Arabica is sweeter and more acidic than Robusta, with fruit, floral and chocolate notes and a medium body. Almost all specialty coffee is Arabica, and most of what you buy as whole bean is too. The Arabica coffee guide goes deeper on flavor and growing.

2. Robusta

Robusta (Coffea canephora) makes up most of the rest of the world crop, close to 40%. The plant shrugs off disease and heat and grows well at low altitude, which is how it got its name. Vietnam and Brazil grow most of it. The beans are smaller and rounder than Arabica and carry roughly twice the caffeine.

Robusta varieties are mostly grouped as Erecta (upright) or Nganda (spreading). The cup is earthy, woody and more bitter, which is why Robusta shows up in instant coffee and supermarket blends. In a good espresso blend, a share of Robusta adds body and a thicker crema.

3. Liberica

Liberica (Coffea liberica) is rare. Together with Excelsa it makes up only around 2% of the world’s coffee. It grows on trees that can reach 20 meters, and the beans are long and irregular, with a hooked tip at one end. It carries less caffeine than Robusta.

Liberica tastes smoky and woody with a fruity, almost jackfruit note, and a full body closer to Robusta than Arabica. You’ll mostly meet it in the Philippines, where it’s sold as barako, and in Malaysia. The Liberica coffee guide has more on its flavor.

4. Excelsa

Excelsa was long treated as a fourth species. Botanists now class it as a variety of Liberica (Coffea liberica var. dewevrei), though growers and roasters still sell it under its own name. Like Liberica it grows on tall trees, but its beans are smaller and teardrop shaped.

Grown well, Excelsa tastes tart and fruity, with a dark, roasty edge. Most of it is blended in for complexity rather than drunk on its own. The Excelsa coffee guide covers where it grows.

Light to dark: the four roast levels

Roast level shapes the cup as much as the bean does. It means how long the green beans spent in the roaster. The longer the roast, the more the origin flavors give way to roast flavors, and the better suited the coffee is to some brew methods over others.

1. Light roast

Light roast comes off the heat soon after the first crack. It keeps the most acidity and the most origin character, so it’s the roast for the fruit and floral notes specialty roasters chase. The beans are pale brown with no oil on the surface. Light roasts are at their best in a pour over.

2. Medium roast

Medium roast is the balanced middle. It’s sweeter and rounder than a light roast, with nutty notes, milder acidity and a hint of bitterness. The beans are medium brown, still with no surface oil, and smell stronger. This is the classic everyday roast for drip machines, and it makes a good cold brew.

3. Medium-dark roast

Medium-dark roast has a heavier body and deep chocolate and nut flavors with a bittersweet finish. Acidity is low. The beans are dark brown, and the first sheen of oil shows on the surface. It suits a French press, a moka pot or espresso.

4. Dark roast

Dark roast is smoky, bitter and heavy, with toasty, chocolatey flavors and very little acidity. Roast flavor dominates, so differences between origins mostly fade. The beans are near black and shiny with oil. Traditional Italian espresso blends are roasted this dark.

Roast level barely changes caffeine. For the roast names you’ll see on bags (City, Full City, Vienna, French, Italian), see the guide to coffee roasts.

Whole bean, ground or instant

Once roasted, coffee is sold in three forms. Whole beans keep their flavor longest, because the aroma escapes fast once the bean is broken. Ground coffee is ready to brew but goes stale within a couple of weeks of opening.

Ground coffee also comes in different grind sizes, and each brew method needs its own. From coarsest to finest, it runs extra coarse for cold brew, coarse for a French press, medium for drip machines and most pour overs, fine for espresso and moka pots, and powder-fine for Turkish coffee. The coffee grind chart has the full list.

Instant coffee is brewed coffee that has been dried into crystals (freeze-dried) or powder (spray-dried), so it dissolves in hot water. It’s quick, mostly made from Robusta, and flatter in flavor. The differences are laid out in instant vs ground coffee.

How it’s brewed

Brew method is the third meaning of the word. Every method uses one of four principles. These are pressure (espresso), immersion (French press, cold brew), filtration (drip, pour over) or decoction, where the coffee is boiled with the water (Turkish coffee). The complete guide to brewing methods covers them all; these are the ones most people mean.

1. Drip coffee

Drip coffee is made in an automatic drip machine. The machine heats water and drips it over grounds in a paper filter, giving a clean, mild, consistent cup. It lacks some of the complexity of other methods, but it takes no skill and makes a whole pot at once, which is why it’s still the default at home in North America.

2. Pour over

Pour over is drip coffee done by hand, with a dripper such as a Hario V60 or a Chemex. You control the pour and the timing, which gives a brighter, more detailed cup with a clean finish. It’s the method that shows off a light roast best. More in the pour over guide.

3. Espresso

Espresso forces hot water, at around 90 to 96°C (195 to 205°F), through finely ground coffee at about 9 bars of pressure. A standard shot runs 25 to 30 seconds at roughly 1 part coffee to 2 parts liquid. The result is small, strong and syrupy, with crema on top, and it’s the base for most café milk drinks. Variations on the shot include the ristretto, the lungo and the doppio, which is a double.

4. French press and moka pot

A French press steeps coarse grounds in hot water for about four minutes, then a metal mesh plunger holds them back. The cup is full-bodied, with more oils and a little sediment. The guide to using a French press has the ratio.

A moka pot sits on the stove and pushes boiling water up through the grounds with steam pressure. It makes a strong, dark coffee that’s close to espresso in strength but without the crema.

5. Cold brew

Cold brew is made with cold water instead of hot. Coarse grounds steep in cold or room-temperature water for 12 to 24 hours, then get strained. It tastes smooth, sweet and low in acidity, and you don’t need special equipment, and a mason jar works.

Don’t confuse it with iced coffee, which is hot-brewed coffee poured over ice. The two taste quite different. Cold brew vs iced coffee compares them.

The classic coffee drinks

Most café menus are built from espresso, brewed coffee, milk and water in different proportions. What separates one drink from the next is the ratio of coffee to water, the ratio of coffee to milk, the texture of the milk, and any extras. For the full list of 60-plus drinks, see the coffee drinks guide.

1. Espresso

The espresso machine was born in Turin, where Angelo Moriondo patented a bulk brewer in 1884. Luigi Bezzera patented a single-serve machine in 1901, and La Pavoni put it into production in 1905.

A single espresso is about 30 ml of strong, concentrated coffee. Drink it as it is, or use it as the base for every milk drink below.

2. Ristretto and lungo

A ristretto is a “restricted” shot. It uses the same dose of coffee pulled with about half the water, so it’s shorter, sweeter and more syrupy. A lungo goes the other way, with roughly twice the water, giving a longer, thinner and more bitter cup. More in ristretto vs espresso and what is a lungo.

3. Americano

An americano is a shot of espresso topped up with hot water. The usual story is that American soldiers in Italy during World War II watered down local espresso to make it last longer and taste more like the coffee at home. It’s less intense than espresso but has more depth than drip coffee. How to make an americano has the steps.

4. Red eye

A red eye is a cup of drip coffee with a shot of espresso added. It’s named after overnight “red-eye” flights, for anyone who needs the extra caffeine. It’s served black. Add two shots and it’s a black eye; three makes a dead eye. The red eye coffee guide has more.

5. Macchiato

Macchiato means “stained” or “marked” in Italian. The story goes that baristas added a spot of milk foam to an espresso so waiters could tell it apart from a plain shot. An espresso macchiato is a single shot with a small spoonful of foam, so it still tastes mostly of espresso. Recipes vary from café to café. Its milkier cousin is the latte macchiato, where a shot is poured into a glass of steamed milk. More on the original in what is an espresso macchiato.

6. Cortado

A cortado is equal parts espresso and steamed milk, with little or no foam, served in a small glass. It comes from Spain, where cortar means “to cut”, and the milk cuts the espresso’s strength. Some US cafés call it a Gibraltar, after the glass it’s served in. It’s strong but well balanced. The cortado recipe shows how to make one.

7. Cappuccino

The cappuccino takes its name from the brown robes of Capuchin friars. Viennese cafés served a “Kapuziner” coffee with cream in the 1700s, but the espresso-based cappuccino appears in Italian records from the 1930s. In Italy it’s a breakfast drink; elsewhere people drink it all day.

A classic cappuccino is one part espresso, one part steamed milk and one part milk foam, often dusted with cocoa or cinnamon. The thick foam makes it lighter in texture than a latte. Compare the three in cappuccino vs latte vs macchiato.

8. Flat white

The flat white comes from Australia and New Zealand, and both countries claim it, with the earliest café records from Sydney and Wellington in the 1980s. It’s a double shot of espresso topped with steamed milk and a thin layer of velvety microfoam, in a smaller cup than a latte. Less milk means a stronger coffee taste than a latte.

9. Latte

Coffee with milk is centuries old, but the latte as a menu item dates from the 1950s at Caffe Mediterraneum in Berkeley, California, and spread through Seattle’s cafés in the 1980s. A latte is a shot of espresso with plenty of steamed milk and a thin layer of foam. With more milk than a cappuccino, it’s sweeter and creamier. Learn how to make a latte at home.

10. Mocha

A mocha, or caffè mocha, is a latte with chocolate. A shot of espresso is mixed with chocolate sauce or cocoa, then topped with steamed milk and often whipped cream. It’s sweet and rich, closer to a hot chocolate with a coffee kick. See how it compares in mocha vs latte.

11. Café au lait

Café au lait is French for “coffee with milk”, and it dates back to the cafés of late 17th-century Paris. In France it’s strong brewed coffee with hot milk, often served in a bowl at breakfast. In the US it’s usually drip coffee topped with steamed milk. Either way it tastes milder than a latte, because the base is brewed coffee rather than espresso.

12. Irish coffee

Irish coffee is hot coffee with Irish whiskey, brown sugar and a layer of lightly whipped cream floating on top. Chef Joe Sheridan created it in 1943 at the flying-boat terminal in Foynes, County Limerick, for passengers whose transatlantic flight had turned back in bad weather. It’s sweet and warming, with strong coffee and whiskey flavors. The Irish coffee recipe shows how to make it.

13. Turkish coffee

Turkish coffee reached Istanbul around the 1540s from Yemen, and coffee houses spread through the Ottoman Empire within a few decades. It’s both a brew method and a drink. Powder-fine coffee, water and usually sugar are heated together in a small pot called a cezve or ibrik until it foams, then served unfiltered. The grounds settle in the cup, and the coffee is thick, strong and sweet. You’ll need a Turkish coffee grinder to get the grind fine enough.

14. Italian coffee

“Italian coffee” usually means the espresso style Italy made famous. That is a dark roast blend, pulled short and strong, with a heavy body. Italian blends mix Arabica with a share of Robusta for crema and body. The coffee itself can come from anywhere; the roast and the style make it Italian. The best Italian coffee brands page has examples.

15. Iced coffee and cold brew

Iced coffee is hot-brewed coffee cooled over ice. It keeps more of the brightness of hot coffee but can taste watery if the ice melts too fast. Cold brew, covered above, is smoother and less acidic. Both can take milk, syrup or both. Any espresso drink can be served iced too, the iced latte being the most common.

Where to start

If you’re new to all this, start with the bean and the roast, because they set the flavor before any brewing happens. A medium roast Arabica brewed in a drip machine or French press is the safe middle ground. If you like bright, fruity coffee, go lighter and try a pour over. If you like it bold and bitter, go darker and try espresso or a moka pot. Then use the drinks list to decide how much milk you want with it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the four main types of coffee?

If you mean beans, they are Arabica, Robusta, Liberica and Excelsa. Arabica and Robusta make up almost all the coffee traded worldwide. If you mean drinks, the four most ordered are espresso, americano, cappuccino and latte.

How many types of coffee are there?

There are four bean species in commercial use, four main roast levels, around half a dozen common home brew methods, and dozens of named drinks. This page covers the 16 drinks you'll see on most café menus.

Which type of coffee is the strongest?

For caffeine, Robusta beans carry roughly twice as much as Arabica, and espresso is the most concentrated brew by volume, though a large mug of drip coffee holds more caffeine in total than a single shot. For flavor intensity, a dark roast brewed as espresso or Turkish coffee is about as heavy as coffee gets.

Does the roast level change the caffeine?

Barely. Light and dark roasts have almost the same caffeine bean for bean. Any difference comes from how you measure: a scoop of light roast is denser and holds slightly more coffee by weight, while a scoop of dark roast holds slightly less.

What is the most popular type of coffee?

By bean, Arabica. By drink, drip coffee is still the everyday cup at home in North America, while espresso drinks like the latte and cappuccino lead café menus.
