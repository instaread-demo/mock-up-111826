Ground stop exposes capitalism’s infrastructure failings

WW editorial

By Editor posted on November 17, 2026

Workers have protested federal layoffs, which have led to infrastructure crises such as the recent ground stop. Cleveland, Feb. 20, 2025. WW Photo: Martha Grevatt

Nov. 16 — On November 13 the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) declared a “ground stop,” meaning all air travel across the U.S. was halted. It lasted four hours, disrupting travel for more than 1.5 million travelers. Days later, some people are still stuck at airports trying to reschedule their flights.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, whose responsibilities include overseeing the FAA, said the ground stop was caused by the replacement of aging, obsolete copper wiring with fiber optic cables — a process that has dragged on for decades. This work reportedly triggered a cascading network failure. 

This was only the third countrywide ground stop in the entire history of aviation in the U.S. The first was in 2001 after the Sept. 11 attacks on the World Trade Center, the Pentagon and the hijacked flight in Pennsylvania presumably bound for the White House or the Capitol. The second, in 2023, was due to a failure of the Notice to Air Missions system that lasted 90 minutes.

President Donald Trump, who appointed Duffy, was silent on the crisis for two days. His selection for Transportation Secretary had no prior experience with or interest in the transportation field before the appointment was approved by the Senate in 2025. Most recently Duffy was a program host on Fox TV, having earlier been a U.S. representative from Wisconsin.

Duffy’s FAA put out a statement on the ground stop designed to reassure the U.S. public that this was nothing to worry about. It said that “safety is always our top priority” and multiple times claimed the FAA action was due to an “abundance of caution.”

However, the situation raises more questions than it answers. Why, after decades of slow work, is outmoded technology still being replaced? Why are air traffic controllers forced to work with outdated equipment? Why is the number of controllers going down while the number of flights increases?

Firing of striking PATCO members in 1981

The crisis of air traffic control in the U.S. has existed for decades. On Aug. 5, 1981, then-President Ronald Reagan fired all striking members of the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO). The key issue in the strike, safety — on-the-job stress — prompted demands for a 32-hour work week. 

The breaking of the PATCO strike led to the dissolution of the union. By 1987 air traffic controllers hired to replace the strikers were dealing with the same issues that prompted the 1981 strike, and they organized a new union, National Air Traffic Controllers Association (NATCA). After this latest ground stop, NATCA called for adequate funding to allow its members to do their jobs properly.

Before the crushing of PATCO there were 16,375 air traffic controllers. Today there are just over 11,000 with about 4,000 more still in training. They are working 10-hour days, six days per week. Yet there are some 44,000 flights per day in the U.S., far more than in 1981. (faa.gov)

The real problem is the federal government’s funding priorities. Funds are lacking for critical infrastructure updates, and federal workers across the board have been laid off. Yet there is funding for the repressive state apparatus, including for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) thugs who terrorize immigrants regardless of their legal status. There is funding for the U.S.-Israeli war against Iran.

Passengers try to rebook flights, Nov. 13, 2026, Denver.

Under capitalism, people’s needs take a back seat to securing profits for the wealthy class. Infrastructure suffers. The ground stop is just the latest example; another recent infrastructure failure was the building collapse in Brooklyn, New York, on Nov. 11, which left at least eight people dead. Then, on the same day as the FAA’s ground stop, an asphalt tanker rammed  a docked cruise ship in New Orleans. These are just the most recent examples.

A profit-driven system provides little to no incentive for maintaining infrastructure or enforcing safety rules. It is working-class and oppressed people who always pay the price for the failings of capitalism. All of the latest crises demonstrate the need for a socialist solution.
