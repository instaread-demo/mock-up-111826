Manuel de Falla’s “The Three-Cornered Hat”: Two Suites from the Ballet

Spanish composer Manuel de Falla’s ballet score, El sombrero de tres picos (“The Three-Cornered Hat”), bathes in the bright colors of a searing Iberian sun. Filled with the infectious rhythms of Spanish dance, the music is sultry, exotic, and at times tantalizingly mysterious.

The Three-Cornered Hat was commissioned by Sergei Diaghilev in 1917 and premiered two years later in London by the Ballets Russes. It expanded on de Falla’s two-scene pantomime, The Magistrate and the Miller’s Wife, based on an 1874 novel by Pedro Antonio de Alarcón. As musicologist John Henke notes, the comic, farcical story involves a love triangle among “a jealous miller, his beautiful young wife, and a lecherous corregidor—the local magistrate, whose position is symbolized by his three-cornered hat.” The buffoonish magistrate attempts to seduce the happily married miller’s wife and ends up arrested in a case of mistaken identity. The action unfolds amid spicy flamenco rhythms and the modal harmony of Andalusian folk music.

Manuel de Falla arranged two orchestral suites from the score. Suite No. 1 opens with a curtain-raising trumpet, horn, and timpani fanfare. The music that follows paints the atmosphere of a sleepy Spanish village on a languid afternoon. The strident song of a blackbird rings out. It is a pet of the miller and his wife, who attempt to teach it to mark the hours of the day. The miller and his wife are introduced by provincial Spanish folk melodies. The bassoon accompanies the ludicrous and eccentric Corregidor.

The “Dance of the Miller’s Wife” is a whirling, seductive fandango, launched into motion by a depiction of rasgueado-style guitar strumming. The miller’s wife teases the Corregidor with a bunch of grapes until he tumbles down. Suite No. 1 ends in high-flying exuberance and a blazing flash of color.

Suite No. 2 opens with “The Neighbors’ Dance,” which depicts a festive celebration of the Feast of St. John. It is the rapid triplet-meter folk dance of the seguidillas. Next comes the ferocious, spirited “Miller’s Dance.” In his memoirs, the choreographer Léonide Massine described his inspiration for this thrilling, rustic dance:

I began by stamping my feet repeatedly and twirling my hands over my head. As the music quickened I did a series of high jumps, ending with a turn in mid-air and a savage stamp of the foot as I landed…The mental image of an enraged bull going into the attack unleashed some inner force which generated power within me…For one moment it seemed as if some other person within me was performing the dance.

Fragments of the ballet’s themes come flooding back in the glittering “Final Dance (Jota).” Accompanied by castanets, the jota is an exuberant fertility dance of Aragonese origin, characterized by bouncing steps and raised arms. Swirling ever faster, it delivers the ultimate euphoric happy ending.

About Timothy Judd

A native of Upstate New York, Timothy Judd has been a member of the Richmond Symphony violin section since 2001. He is a graduate of the Eastman School of Music where he earned the degrees Bachelor of Music and Master of Music, studying with world renowned Ukrainian-American violinist Oleh Krysa.

The son of public school music educators, Timothy Judd began violin lessons at the age of four through Eastman’s Community Education Division. He was a student of Anastasia Jempelis, one of the earliest champions of the Suzuki method in the United States.

A passionate teacher, Mr. Judd has maintained a private violin studio in the Richmond area since 2002 and has been active coaching chamber music and numerous youth orchestra sectionals.
