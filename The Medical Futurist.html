40 Consumer Health Trends To Watch In 2027: AI, Diagnostics And The Future Of Healthcare

Every morning, I spend time analyzing the latest developments in digital health, artificial intelligence and medical technologies. After more than 15 years of doing this […]

Dr. Bertalan Mesko, PhD

6 min | 16 November 2026 · Updated 17 November 2026

Key Takeaways

  • I selected 40 consumer health trends that I believe deserve closer attention in 2027, based on my continuous analysis of digital health and medical technologies.

  • From personal AI health assistants to at-home diagnostics and emerging medical devices, these trends highlight how healthcare is evolving.

  • This is not a list of predictions, but a futures studies approach to identifying the developments that stand out and could shape the future of medicine.

Every morning, I spend time analyzing the latest developments in digital health, artificial intelligence and medical technologies. After more than 15 years of doing this work as The Medical Futurist, I have become increasingly interested in identifying the technological trends that stand out from the enormous amount of daily news, research and announcements.

That is why I put together this infographic featuring 40 consumer health trends I will be watching closely in 2027. These developments range from personal AI health coaches and at-home diagnostics to smart medical devices and entirely new ways of accessing healthcare.

An important distinction: I am not predicting that all 40 trends will become mainstream in 2027. In futures studies, identifying and analyzing trends is one of the ways we systematically explore possible futures. Some of these technologies are already available, others are being tested, and a few will need years of further development. Their inclusion reflects the importance I assign to monitoring their progress.

I divided them into four categories to illustrate how consumer health is evolving and where I expect to see particularly interesting developments.

1. AI & Interfaces: How We Interact With Healthcare Will Change

I believe one of the most important changes in healthcare will be the way we access and interact with medical information.

Personal AI health coaches, patient-side AI agents and AI-connected personal health records could gradually give patients access to services that previously required multiple applications, professionals or institutions. Imagine an AI assistant that understands your medical history, helps interpret your wearable data, prepares questions for your physician and assists with navigating healthcare services.

For physicians, large language models could become interfaces to hospital information systems, while ambient AI scribes take over much of the documentation burden. AI scheduling agents and medical concierge services could also simplify the administrative side of healthcare.

I am particularly interested in multimodal medical AI assistants, which can work with text, images, audio and other types of health information. Combined with technologies such as vocal biomarkers or AI cough analysis, they might eventually make certain aspects of health assessment accessible through everyday devices.

The key challenge will be establishing which of these applications are clinically reliable and how they can be integrated into existing healthcare systems. The technology is advancing quickly, but medical validation and appropriate safeguards remain essential.

2. Diagnostics & Monitoring: Healthcare Is Moving Into Everyday Life

A growing number of technologies allow people to measure aspects of their health outside traditional medical settings.

We already have continuous glucose monitors, smart rings, connected respiratory devices and smartphone-based health applications. The next generation of consumer health technologies could make monitoring more comprehensive, convenient and increasingly passive.

At-home blood testing, earables, multi-biomarker wearable sensors and contactless vital sign monitoring are particularly interesting developments. Even smart toilets could potentially become a source of longitudinal health data without requiring users to change their daily routines.

I also included consumer digital twins in this category. These are digital representations that could combine different sources of personal health information to model aspects of an individual’s health and potentially support more personalized decisions.

However, collecting more health data does not automatically lead to better health.

I think the most important question for 2027 will be how these technologies turn measurements into reliable, clinically meaningful information. We need better evidence about accuracy, usefulness and the consequences of constantly monitoring ourselves.

I am especially interested in technologies that can help people make informed decisions while reducing the need for unnecessary medical visits.

3. Consumer Care Platforms: Healthcare Services Are Being Redesigned

The third category focuses on how healthcare services themselves are changing.

Digital front door services can simplify how patients find care, book appointments and communicate with healthcare organizations. Smart medication management tools and digital therapeutics could increasingly support patients between medical visits.

Some developments reflect very specific healthcare needs. GLP-1 medications have created demand for digital lifestyle companions, while menopause technologies address an area of women’s health that has historically received insufficient attention.

Personalized sleep optimization is another area worth following, especially as wearables and health applications become more capable of connecting sleep measurements with daily habits.

One trend I find particularly interesting is gyms evolving into longevity centers. Fitness providers are increasingly interested in combining exercise with diagnostics, health monitoring and personalized prevention services. It raises questions about where the boundaries between wellness and healthcare will be drawn.

Hospital-at-home programs and virtual wards represent a similar change from the perspective of healthcare institutions. Certain services that traditionally required a hospital stay may increasingly be delivered at home, supported by connected devices and remote clinical supervision.

I will be watching how these models demonstrate clinical value and whether healthcare systems can make them accessible beyond small pilot programs.

4. Emerging Devices & Technologies: New Tools For Patients And Physicians

The final category contains some of the most exciting technological developments, although many are still at relatively early stages.

Robotic blood draw devices, needle-free blood sampling and vein visualization technologies could make common medical procedures easier or more accessible. Smart bandages and biosensing patches could support monitoring without requiring repeated manual measurements.

AI-powered portable ultrasound is another technology I find particularly promising. As medical imaging devices become smaller and software takes over more of the interpretation process, diagnostic capabilities could become available in settings where they are currently difficult to access.

I am also following cuffless blood pressure monitoring, smart glasses for healthcare and medical robotics beyond surgical applications.

And I included two developments that represent very different possibilities: menstrual blood-based diagnostics and multi-diagnostic devices often described as medical tricorders. Both are worth monitoring because they explore new approaches to obtaining medical information, although their clinical maturity differs considerably.

These technologies will have to prove that they can deliver reliable results and meaningful benefits compared with existing alternatives. Some may remain specialized tools, while others could eventually become part of everyday healthcare.

What Will I Be Watching Most Closely In 2027?

AI is gradually becoming an interface between people and medical information. Diagnostic technologies are moving beyond hospitals and clinics. Healthcare services are becoming more personalized and accessible through digital platforms. And medical devices are getting smaller, more connected and increasingly supported by software.

These developments could significantly change the relationship between patients, healthcare professionals and healthcare institutions. What interests me most is how these trends begin to interact. A personal AI health assistant connected to continuous monitoring devices and a personal health record could be far more useful than any of those technologies operating independently.

I will also be paying close attention to evidence of clinical value, regulatory progress, actual adoption and whether these technologies become useful in everyday healthcare.

This infographic represents the trends that stand out to me from the constant flow of developments in digital health. I will keep monitoring them, questioning their progress and adjusting my expectations as new evidence becomes available.

After all, preparing for the future of medicine requires us to continuously examine how it is unfolding.
