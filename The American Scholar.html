Asturias Days

The Right Kind

By Clellan Coe | November 18, 2026
Flickr/flowcomm

Instaread

There are two kinds of people, as everyone knows, the good kind and the bad. We might forget this clear dichotomy, but an encounter with a dog reminds you, because a dog will know which kind you are and treat you accordingly. I, for example, must be the good kind, because dogs treat me well. They like me, and they let me pat them or tickle them under the chin. In consequence I do not worry when approaching one on the street whether it bites or doesn’t, because I assume this dog—like its fellows—knows that I am the good kind of person. The dog will smile on me, one good fellow to another.

Returning from a Sunday morning run, I came huffing up the hill. Reaching the narrow lane at the top, I slowed to a walk. I had worn myself out, and walking the last half mile under the overhanging trees felt deserved. I plucked a couple of pieces of passionflower fruit from a vine hanging over a fence and ate them. I figured they must be rich in something good for you. A little farther on, I paused to take in the view of the fields below, where the walking path wound alongside the river. Farther still, I kicked at the first fallen chestnuts, but decided they were too small to be promising.

When I looked up, I saw a woman coming toward me with two dogs. She noticed me too and clipped a lead on the dog at her side, a fluffy, black dog, medium-sized, maybe a lab and collie mix. A minute later, almost even, we said hi, as one does. The black dog tugged in my direction. “Easy,” she said to the dog. It looked up at her, then turned to me again. I stopped and put my hand out so it could sniff my fingers. It stepped closer and pointedly sniffed my bare leg. “Well!” the woman said. The dog twisted its head to give me a searching look before it stuck out its tongue and took a few licks just above my knee.

The woman was embarrassed, and she drew back on the leash. “It’s fine,” I said. “Really.”

She was laughing uncomfortably as she pulled the dog away. “Well, goodbye,” she said, tugging at the dog.

That’s when the other dog came up. It was smaller and yellow, some kind of pug, and had been hovering behind the woman. “You want to say hi, too?” I said, bending to pet it. With no fear or doubt, it stepped right up to me and commenced to lick my calf.

The owner seemed floored. Both dogs! Without so much as a by-your-leave! To a stranger! She shook her head and said, “Look at that!” As if lightning had struck twice.

In Alice Munro’s short story “Eskimo,” a character named Mary Jo looks away from her screen on a long flight and sees the woman across the aisle licking her companion’s face, kissing his eyelids and mouth. A disturbing sight. Disturbing and distasteful, Mary Jo thinks, unable to look away. She imagines the girl’s thick tongue and is almost sick with revulsion at the weird, appalling spectacle of the girl licking the man’s face all over—his nose, his cheek, his neck and chin. Not greedily, but in devotion. The man permits it. Then, still in a trance of devotion, the girl takes a breath, and resumes. I felt Mary Jo’s disgust. Ugh.

But let a horse lick my fingers or a dog lick my leg, and I feel no squeamishness at all. I told the woman on the path, “Must be the salt.” I could feel it drying on my skin. The blond dog gave me another lick as its owner and I said goodbye.

Then a parting lick. Or was it a kiss? The owner had turned away from the sight of her dogs—bathing and caressing me. Even getting a taste of me. Not simply good or bad, after all, but good, bad, or deliciously salty.
