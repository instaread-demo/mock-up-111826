The lender with the lowest advertised rate might require you to borrow more than you need. Another might waive a fee but charge more interest over the life of the loan. The better offer depends on how much cash you need, what you’ll pay upfront, and how long you expect to carry the debt.

We compared five lenders that offer fixed-rate home equity loans. The reviews cover loan amounts, fees, credit score requirements, and property rules so you can narrow your choices before you request quotes.

5 Best Home Equity Loan Lenders

Not all home equity loans are the same. Some lenders offer lower interest rates, fewer fees, or faster funding times, making them a better fit depending on your financial needs. We’ve reviewed the top home equity loan providers based on loan terms, borrowing limits, and overall customer experience to help you find the best option.

Rocket Mortgage

Crediful’s rating
Learn More

Rocket Mortgage is worth a look if you need a larger loan and have a strong credit score.

It offers fixed-rate terms of 10 or 20 years and loans from $45,000 to $500,000 in most states. The minimum is $10,000 for properties in Michigan. You can apply even if another lender holds your first mortgage.

Rocket spells out how your FICO credit score affects how much you can borrow. Its maximum combined loan-to-value ratio is 80% at a credit score of 680, 85% at a credit score of 700, and 90% at a credit score of 740.

The ratio measures the debt against your home after you add the new loan. Rocket also requires a debt-to-income ratio of 50% or less and documentation of your income and assets.

Rocket currently advertises a waiver of its $795 origination fee on eligible retail loans if you lock the rate on or after August 25, 2026. Ask whether the offer applies and what other closing costs you’d pay. Rocket also advertises funding in as few as 15 days.

Better.com

Better offers fixed-rate home equity loans of up to $500,000 for eligible primary residences, second homes, and investment properties. Its product disclosure excludes Minnesota.

The maximum depends on your home’s value, the debt you already owe against it, and your finances. Better publishes a 30-year loan example, but your quote will show which terms and fees apply to your property.

Better’s online process may help if you need the money quickly and qualify for an automated valuation.

The lender says it can fund in as few as seven days from application if the loan is under $400,000, you upload the required documents within 24 hours, and you can close at the earliest appointment with a notary. An in-person appraisal or a later closing appointment can extend that timeline.

Better may let you add closing costs to your loan balance. If you take that option, ask how much cash you’ll receive and how much interest you’ll pay on the higher balance.

Better also offers a home equity line of credit, so ask for a quote on the fixed-rate home equity loan when you contact them.

Navy Federal Credit Union

Navy Federal offers fixed-rate home equity loans from $10,000 to $500,000. You can choose a term of 5, 10, 15, or 20 years.

The credit union covers closing costs such as appraisal, title, and recording charges. It doesn’t charge application or origination fees. You may still owe prepaid interest or need to make an initial escrow deposit.

Navy Federal lets eligible borrowers access up to 100% of their home equity, subject to its loan limits and approval.

If you borrow the full amount of your equity, your mortgage and new loan together could equal your home’s value. That leaves little room for a drop in value or the cost of selling. If you expect to move soon, estimate what you’d keep after you pay both loans and cover your selling costs.

You’ll need to qualify for membership. Eligibility extends to service members, veterans, certain Department of Defense personnel, and qualifying family or household members.

The credit union also services its fixed-rate equity loans for the life of the loan, so you can go back to the same institution with payment questions.

U.S. Bank lets you start online, call a banker, or visit a branch. It also lets you check whether you prequalify without affecting your credit score.

If you’d rather ask a banker to walk through the fees and payment options with you, check whether the bank offers home equity loans in your area.

U.S. Bank lists loan amounts from $25,000 to $500,000 and terms up to 30 years. It requires a FICO credit score of at least 660 and lists an 80% loan-to-value limit for its home equity loan. Its advertised rate examples use stricter credit and equity assumptions than the minimum requirements for approval. Ask for a quote that reflects your loan amount and credit score.

The bank offers a no-closing-cost option on eligible loans. Its disclosures say some fees may still apply, so ask which costs the bank covers and which you’d pay. The lowest advertised rate requires automatic payments from an eligible U.S. Bank checking or savings account.

TD Bank offers loans from $10,000 on eligible primary residences. That can help if a repair or renovation falls below another lender’s minimum.

If you want to borrow against a second home or investment property, TD requires at least $25,000. Its fixed-rate loan terms range from 5 to 30 years, with options that depend on the property and loan.

TD charges a $99 origination fee. Other closing costs may apply to loans over $500,000 or loans on co-ops or investment properties.

If you plan to sell or refinance soon, ask what you’d pay to record the lien release when you repay the loan. TD offers a rate discount of 0.25 percentage points if you set up automatic payments from an eligible TD checking or savings account. Ask what happens to the discount if you stop those payments.

Check your property’s location before you apply. TD says a home equity application can take 30 to 45 days to reach a final decision. You’ll still need to close before you receive the money.

How We Chose These Home Equity Loan Lenders

We reviewed the five lenders’ published terms and disclosures on November 11, 2026. We focused on fixed-rate loans that pay out a lump sum. We compared loan sizes, repayment terms, closing costs, credit score requirements, and restrictions that could rule out a borrower or property.

Each pick serves a different need. A lender with a lower minimum can help you borrow only what your project costs. A lender with a higher equity limit may help when your current mortgage takes up more of your home’s value. Check the terms for your own property and loan amount before you choose.

Home Equity Loan Amounts, Terms, and Fees

A home equity loan gives you a lump sum and a fixed monthly principal and interest payment. If you already have a mortgage, you can keep its rate and repayment schedule. Your home secures the new loan, so the lender can foreclose if you can’t repay it.

Start with lenders that offer the amount you need for your type of property. A $15,000 project, a loan against a rental, and a renovation that requires most of your equity may each call for a different lender.

How Much Can You Borrow With a Home Equity Loan?

Start with your home’s estimated value and the balances on any loans against it. If your home is worth $300,000 and your mortgage balance is $200,000, you have $100,000 in home equity. The lender’s combined loan-to-value limit determines how much of that equity you can borrow.

Suppose the lender caps your combined loan-to-value ratio at 80%. Multiply $300,000 by 80% to get $240,000. That’s the maximum total debt the lender would allow against the property. Subtract the $200,000 mortgage and the potential new loan comes to $40,000 before fees. Any other debt the lender counts against the home can reduce that amount.

Check the result against the lender’s minimum loan amount. In this example, you could have enough equity for a $30,000 project but still fall short of a lender’s $45,000 minimum. If you already have a home equity line of credit, ask the lender how it will count that line when it calculates your limit.

Use this math to build your shortlist. The lender’s valuation, your income, and your credit history can change the amount you qualify for.

Home Equity Loan Requirements

Two homeowners with the same amount of equity can get different offers. Lenders also consider credit history, income, existing payments, and the property itself.

Credit Score and Credit History

Check the lender’s minimum credit score and ask which credit score model it uses. The credit score you see in a banking app can differ from the one a lender uses to approve your loan. Review your credit report for incorrect balances or accounts you don’t recognize, especially if you’re close to the lender’s cutoff.

Some lenders use credit score tiers to set how much you can borrow or how much you’ll pay. If a lender offers a soft credit inquiry to check your rate or prequalify, ask what information it can give you before you submit a full application.

Income and Debt-to-Income Ratio

Lenders divide your required monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income to calculate your debt-to-income ratio. If you earn $8,000 a month before taxes and pay $2,800 toward debt, a new $600 loan payment would bring the total to $3,400. That works out to a debt-to-income ratio of 42.5%.

Each lender sets its own limits. If you plan to use the loan to pay off debt, ask which payments the lender will include and whether it requires those accounts to be paid at closing. If your income comes from self-employment, commissions, or rent, ask what records the lender needs to count it.

Property Value and Ownership

The lender needs to confirm what your home is worth and which loans or other claims already appear on the title. Tell the lender early if you own the home with someone else, hold it in a trust, or rent it out. Those details can affect eligibility and which documents you’ll need.

Ask how the lender will value the property. Some loans qualify for an automated valuation. Other loans require an appraisal, which can add cost and time. Find out what you’ll owe if you pay for an appraisal and the loan doesn’t close.

How to Compare Home Equity Loan Offers

Ask at least two lenders for quotes for the same loan amount and term. Try to get them around the same time because rates can change. If one lender adds fees to the balance or deducts them from your proceeds, check that both offers would leave you with enough cash for the expense.

Interest Rate, APR, and Closing Costs

The interest rate determines how much interest you pay on the balance. The annual percentage rate includes the interest rate and certain loan charges. Compare both figures, then look at the itemized costs on each Loan Estimate. Pay attention to origination charges, appraisal and title fees, and any points you’d pay for a lower rate.

Say you’re comparing two $50,000 loans with 10-year terms. At 7.5%, you’d pay about $1,576 less in interest over 10 years than you would at 8%. If the 7.5% offer costs $2,000 more in upfront fees, you’d still spend about $425 more over the full term. Ask for quotes with and without points if the lender gives you that choice.

Look at cash due at closing separately. Some of that amount may go toward prepaid expenses such as interest, taxes, or insurance. If a lender offers a credit or a no-closing-cost option, compare the rate and balance with the quote you’d get if you paid the fees yourself.

Monthly Payments and Total Interest

On a $50,000 loan at a fixed 8% interest rate, a 10-year term costs $606.64 a month in principal and interest. You’d pay about $22,797 in interest over the full term, before fees.

A 20-year term at the same rate costs $418.22 a month. The payment drops by about $188, but total interest rises to about $50,373. Put the new payment alongside your current mortgage, taxes, insurance, and other bills before you decide which term fits your budget.

If you expect to sell or refinance within five years, compare how much each loan will cost over the time you’ll keep it. On page 3 of the Loan Estimate, look for the comparison figures for the first five years. Subtract the principal you’d repay from the total you’d pay over that period to see the interest and fees. Also ask whether you’d owe a charge if you pay off the loan early.

How to Apply for a Home Equity Loan

Ask the lender for its document list before you submit your application. Find out when it checks your credit, whether you can lock the rate, and when the rate lock expires.

Gather Your Financial and Property Documents

Start with your mortgage statement, proof of income, and homeowners insurance details. The lender may ask for pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns, or bank statements. If you already have a second mortgage or home equity line of credit, include the latest statement so the lender can account for it.

Tell the lender how much cash you need after fees. If your contractor’s quote is $35,000, check whether a $35,000 loan would deliver that amount or whether some costs would come out of the proceeds.

Schedule the Property Valuation

Once the lender reviews your application, it’ll tell you what kind of valuation it needs. If it requires an appraisal, ask when the appraiser can visit and how long the lender expects to take to review it. The valuation can affect your rate or loan amount, so leave time to adjust your plans if the home comes in below your estimate.

Review the Closing Disclosure

Compare the Closing Disclosure with your Loan Estimate before you sign. Check the rate, monthly payment, loan balance, fees, and the amount of cash you’ll receive. Ask the lender to explain any changes you didn’t expect, especially if it added closing costs to the balance or changed the amount due at closing.

Find out who will collect your payments and when the first payment is due. If your rate discount depends on automatic payments, confirm which account you’ll use and when the first withdrawal will happen.

Confirm the Funding Date

For most home equity loans against your primary residence, federal rules give you three business days to cancel after you sign and receive the required disclosures and cancellation notice. Saturdays count. Sundays and federal holidays don’t. If you sign and get all the documents on a Friday, you generally have until midnight Tuesday to cancel, assuming there’s no federal holiday in between.

The lender typically waits until the cancellation period ends to send the funds. Ask for the expected transfer date before you commit to a contractor’s schedule or promise a creditor a payment.

When a Home Equity Loan Makes Sense

A home equity loan fits an expense with a price you already know. If you have a firm bid for a roof replacement or a renovation, you can borrow the amount you need and budget for the payment before work begins. Make sure you can afford the loan even if the project runs over budget or the home doesn’t increase in value.

If you itemize deductions, you may be able to deduct interest when you use the money to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures the loan. Mortgage debt limits and other IRS rules apply. Keep records of how you spend the proceeds if you plan to claim a deduction.

If you want to consolidate credit cards, compare what you’d pay in interest and fees under both repayment plans. A smaller monthly payment can cost more if you stretch repayment over a longer term. You’d also give the new lender a claim against your home to pay off debt that didn’t use your home as collateral. Before you take the loan, make a plan to keep the card balances from building back up.

Home Equity Loan Alternatives

If your project cost could change or you’ll pay for the work in stages, compare a home equity line of credit. A HELOC typically lets you borrow as needed during a draw period. Many lines have variable rates, so check how much you could owe if the rate rises or the repayment period begins.

Some HELOCs have different draw rules. Figure’s HELOC requires a full draw at funding. You may be able to borrow again as you repay principal, subject to the line’s terms. If you only need a small part of the credit limit at first, ask how the upfront draw would affect your interest costs.

PenFed’s HELOC gives eligible borrowers the option to lock a fixed rate on advances of at least $10,000 during the draw period. Ask what rate applies to the rest of the balance and how much you’d pay if you close the line early.

A cash-out refinance replaces your first mortgage with a larger one. It may make sense if you also want to refinance your mortgage. Compare the new rate on the full balance, especially if your current mortgage has a low rate.

For a smaller expense, price an unsecured personal loan as well. The interest rate may be higher, but the fees and shorter repayment term can change the total cost. You also avoid pledging your home as collateral.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a home equity loan if my house is paid off?

Yes, if the lender offers home equity loans on homes without a mortgage and you meet its requirements. The home equity loan would generally take the first lien position. Ask whether the lender has different rates, fees, or loan limits for first-lien home equity loans.

Can I sell my house before my home equity loan is paid off?

Yes. Your closing agent typically pays the home equity loan and any first mortgage from the sale proceeds. Ask for payoff statements so you can estimate what you’ll keep after both loans and selling costs. If the proceeds could fall short, contact your lenders before you accept an offer.

Can I refinance a home equity loan later?

Yes, if you qualify for a new loan that pays off the existing one. Start with the remaining balance and term on your current loan. Compare the new closing costs and interest over the time you expect to keep the loan. A lower rate can still cost you more if you pay new fees and add years to repayment.
