How to Maximize Your Casino Rewards Program

November 4, 2026  in General by Jeff Donchess

Instaread

Before I built DRatings, I spent years in the credit industry. In that world, every card swipe fed a model. The issuer scored your profitability, predicted when you might leave, and decided how much reward value to send back to keep you. Casinos run the same playbook. Your players club card works like a credit file, and a marketing model on the other end decides what you get.

Once you understand how that model thinks, you can feed it the right data. Below, I share how I believe casinos score players and the habits I use to get the most out of any rewards program.

How the Casinos Score You

Everything starts with one number: theoretical loss, or “theo.” The casino multiplies your average bet by decisions per hour, hours played, and the house edge. In other words, theo measures what the casino expects to win from you, not what you actually lost. The casino then returns a slice of that theo as comps.

On top of theo, casinos track recency, frequency, and spend. Credit card marketers use the exact same framework. How much you play sets the size of your offers, while how often and how recently you visit sets their timing. Keep those levers in mind, because every tip below pulls on at least one of them.

Always Show Your Players Club Card

This sounds obvious, yet players skip it all the time. If the card isn’t in the machine or on the table, the model never sees your play. As a result, that session simply doesn’t exist.

At table games, hand the dealer your card and ask to be rated. Then show it everywhere else too: the restaurant, the spa, the gift shop, and the front desk. In credit, we called missing data a “thin file.” Don’t let the casino see a thin file when you actually spend real money there.

Spend Money on the Property

Here’s where the math gets interesting. Most major programs now reward non-gaming spend, and many weight it generously. Caesars Rewards, for example, gives 1 Tier Credit for every $1 spent at participating restaurants but only 1 Tier Credit for every $5 of slot coin-in.

Think about that for a second. A $200 dinner earns the same tier progress as $1,000 run through a slot machine. Better yet, you walk away from dinner with a meal instead of an expected loss.

From a modeling standpoint, the casino values your total wallet, not just your gambling. A guest who books the room, eats the steak dinner, and visits the spa looks like a high-value customer worth keeping. So if you plan to eat anyway, eat on property. Also, book your room directly, since third-party sites can strip the stay out of your file.

Look Like a Losing Player at the Tables

I’ll be honest here: comps run mainly on theo, not results. So why would looking like a loser help?

Real people still make a lot of comp decisions. The pit records your buy-in and what you walk away with, so your file shows wins and losses alongside theo. Hosts and floor supervisors then use judgment on extras. Even more important, a losing player looks like a retention risk to a model.

The practical move: pull chips off the table as you win and put them in your pocket. It’s legal, and it doubles as good bankroll discipline. Still, keep it subtle. Pocket green chips rather than black ones, and skip the theatrics. Pit staff notice more than you think, and overdoing it can backfire.

Tip Well and Treat Employees With Respect

Every scoring model I ever worked with had an override. An underwriter could look at a file and adjust the decision based on judgment. Casinos have overrides too, and real people run them.

At the tables, the floor supervisor estimates your average bet. No machine tracks it. Because that number is an estimate, a friendly relationship can nudge it your way. Likewise, dealers talk to the floor, and hosts talk to everyone. Tip your dealer, tip your server, and say thank you. Then, when you ask a host for a dinner comp or a room upgrade, you’ll already have goodwill banked.

A Few More Ideas

Ask what they have you rated at. Floor staff estimate your average bet, and a polite question at the end of a session can fix an error before it hits your file.

Pick your game wisely. Casinos sometimes rate blackjack at a higher edge than a basic strategy player actually faces. If the casino rates a $25 player at 2% for four hours at 70 hands an hour, that’s $140 of theo. Meanwhile, the player’s true expected loss at a 0.5% edge sits near $35. In that case, the comps can cover a big chunk of the real cost.

Read your mailers. Your offers reveal what the model thinks of you. Rising offers mean your last trip raised your rating, while shrinking offers mean a big trip is aging out of your average.

Never Chase Comps

Let me end with the most important point. Comps return a fraction of what the casino expects to win from you. If you bet more or play longer just to earn a free buffet, you lose that trade every time.

Instead, treat comps as a rebate on trips you already planned. Set your budget first, play your normal game, and use these habits to make sure the model gives you full credit. That’s how a statistician plays the rewards game.
