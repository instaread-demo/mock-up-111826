Targeting Your Law Firm’s Ideal Client

By Barbara Koenen-Geerdink

Companies Don’t Hire Lawyers, People Do

We’ve all had someone try to sell us something we didn’t need. A credit card, an energy contract or a membership we had zero interest in. Did we buy it? Probably not. It didn’t fit life or solve a problem, so why would we?

The same principle applies to legal services. To win work, you need to understand who you’re trying to reach and why they need you. Too many law firm newsletters, seminars and LinkedIn posts fail to land because they don’t answer these questions.

And I can almost hear the response: “But I’m a lawyer. I’m not in sales.” I wouldn’t describe lawyers as salespeople either, but every time a client chooses you over another firm, someone makes a buying decision.

Wouldn’t it help to know who’s making it, and why?

What an Ideal Client Profile Tells You

An ideal client profile, or ICP, describes the types of clients you are in the best position to help and would like more of. For a law firm that’s advising businesses, it usually starts with the organization: its sector, size, location and legal needs. This is knowing your client in a commercial sense, beyond the compliance exercise we associate with KYC.

The word “ideal” matters.

Plenty of companies need a lawyer. Far fewer are a good fit for you. You need to consider whether you can serve the potential client well, whether the work makes commercial sense, and whether you’d want to build a relationship.

Here’s some insight into how the process works.

I’m working with a senior litigation associate who is building his profile and business plan with the ambition of becoming a partner. When I asked who his clients were, he gave me a few company names. When I asked who he wanted his clients to be, he gave a different answer. Some names overlapped, but the prospective clients had something in common: they were in insurance.

His work spanned several industries. When disputes arose, he stepped in to help resolve them. That breadth was valuable experience, but it didn’t give him a clear focus for developing his own practice. He got much of his work through partners. To bring in work himself, he needed to be more deliberate about where he invested his time.

He had a keen interest in insurance, so it made sense to explore that focus.

Your ICP Is a Useful Decision-making Filter

Relationships take time, effort and attention, all of which lawyers have in limited supply. As the old saying goes, try to be known by everyone, and you’ll be remembered by no one.

Once you understand your ICP, it becomes a useful filter for the decisions that follow. Imagine being invited to speak at a conference. Your ego says yes, but your ICP should get a vote first. Ask who will be in the room. Will your ideal clients attend? Will people who influence or refer work to them be there? Does the subject give you a chance to demonstrate expertise they need?

There may be other good reasons to accept, but make it a conscious choice. An invitation alone doesn’t make an event a good business development opportunity.

Personas: Understand the People Making the Hiring Decision

The organization is only part of the picture. You’ll build relationships with people inside it, and those people may have very different priorities. A claims manager, a general counsel and someone in procurement can all influence the choice of external lawyer.

This is where personas help. An ICP describes the organization you want to work with. A persona describes a person involved in buying or using your services: what they’re responsible for, what they need, and how they make decisions.

You may need more than one persona.

The person who first identifies a problem may not control the budget or approve the appointment. If you only understand one of them, you may miss what the others need to feel comfortable instructing you.

In our insurance example, start with a claims manager. Treat what follows as questions to explore, not assumptions about everyone with that job title.

A second framework for personas will help here:

Understanding your audience means taking an interest in the person as well as the professional. Their interests, habits and stage of life can help you understand how they prefer to engage and where you might find common ground. For example, if you notice that many of your clients and prospective clients play pickleball, organizing a tournament could bring them together. Likewise, there’s no point inviting a claims manager to a box at Yankee Stadium if her compliance team won’t let her accept.

Persona mapping is a generalization.

Not everyone shares the same interests or preferences, but recognizing common patterns gives you a better starting point. The value lies in grounding that picture in what you learn through conversations and observation, then adjusting it as you get to know people better.

Know Who You Don’t Want, Too

Knowing your ICP also helps you recognize a poor fit. Expectations about fees, responsiveness and how you work together matter. So does a shared understanding of what good advice looks like. As in any relationship, a persistent mismatch makes things difficult.

A focus doesn’t mean turning away every client outside it. It gives a clearer basis for choosing where to put your business development effort. You can still respond to a good opportunity while being deliberate about the practice you want to build.

Action Steps for Defining Your Law Firm’s Ideal Client

  1. Start by looking at the clients you most enjoy working with and where you do your best work. What do they have in common?
  2. Write down an initial profile, speak to a few people who fit it and use what you learn to decide your next business development activity.
  3. Before committing the time, ask yourself: who am I trying to reach, and why would this matter to them?
