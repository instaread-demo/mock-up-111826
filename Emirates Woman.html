Miuccia Prada embraces retro athleticwear for Miu Miu SS27 at Paris Fashion Week

by Riya Kumar
October 5, 2026
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 2:02
Instaread

Miuccia Prada’s SS27 ready-to-wear collection for Miu Miu swapped last season’s outdoor mood for retro sporty, collegiate dressing with a richer, more polished finish. Presented on the 4th of October at Paris’s Palais d’Iéna, the show unfolded in a gymnasium setting, with a purple court-lined runway setting the scene.

The show opened with the tailoring in heritage checks—boxy tweed and houndstooth blazers layered over cropped jersey tops and pleated skirts, with drawstring waists fastened by rope laces. A red houndstooth jacket, paired with patterned micro shorts and a navy V-neck, gave the preppy mood a cheeky twist.

Suits with glossy python print in brown and mustard yellow were paired with a scarf-style polka-dot cropped top stitched into a halter neck, while the satin blouses were layered beneath a raw-edged leather vest. Leather wraparound minis with high slits and chain-and-grommet belts brought a Western edge, while vintage curtain-style prints appeared across pleated mini dresses.

Everyday dressing remained the heart of the collection—oversized blazers were paired with striped track trousers, and the collection proceeded with crinkled garments as if they were made to live in. Accessories kept the mood playful, from oval sunglasses and snakeskin satchels to buckled loafers, bow-trimmed slingbacks, thong sandals and logo sneakers in green, red and blue. The cast was equally eclectic, with Olivia Colman, Emma Corrin, Jamie Bell and Noah Jupe among those walking the show. When asked about the diverse casting, Prada explained that the aim was to include young faces, older women, men and a range of different characters.

Olivia Colman and Emma Corrin

Together, the looks captured Prada’s ability to make sportswear and tailoring feel elevated without losing their casual spirit. Heritage fabrics met athletic details, delicate crystals met rope laces, and polished tailoring was offset by pieces that felt distinctly easy. The result was playful, wearable and unmistakably Miu Miu, proving once again that the most everyday pieces can still hold plenty of intrigue.

Who Am I? : The UAE women determined to change the conversation around perimenopause

by Sitara Mulchandani
November 18, 2026

Nobody prepared us for this. You turn the radio down so you can see better. You walk into a room and can’t remember why. You forget someone’s name while you are actively talking to them. If you hear the sound of chewing one more time, your blood starts boiling. And your patience has entered witness protection, especially if someone says “calm down”. Welcome to perimenopause – the rollercoaster ride you never knew you signed up for. Women have, of course, been experiencing these symptoms for generations. We just haven’t been particularly good at talking about them. But that is changing. Today, perimenopause and menopause are having a cultural moment. From Davina McCall’s documentaries breaking viewing records in the UK to the HRT (hormone replacement therapy) conversation exploding across social media, a generation of women in their 40s is refusing to suffer in silence. Perimenopause and menopause have finally entered the mainstream conversation. And the UAE is joining in. Perhaps the language itself tells us something about why it has taken the country so long. “In Arabic, menopause literally means سن اليأس (sinn al-ya’s), ‘the age of despair’,” explains Hajar Ouhsine, founder of Menovivre, the UAE’s first dedicated menopause and perimenopause clinic. It is a bleak description for something half the population will eventually experience, and a narrative Ouhsine is determined to change after facing her own challenges with perimenopause.

Her introduction came at just 39. “I was not sharp anymore. It was like something was off. I was always blaming myself. What’s happening? Why am I not focused as before? Why am I always anxious? Why can’t I sleep?” For the next 18 months, Ouhsine saw doctors, ran tests and waited for answers, only to be told it was stress. Then, while listening to Dr Mary Claire Haver’s unPaused podcast one morning, something finally clicked: “She was talking about perimenopause and the symptoms that are not common”, Ouhsine recalls. “I stopped the treadmill, and I said, “I think it’s me!” I had the feeling that I was liberated.”

Yet, finding the answer was only half the battle. Figuring out treatment meant navigating gynaecologists, functional doctors and physiotherapists whose treatment plans often contradicted one another. “They didn’t speak to each other,” she explains, frustrated at the lack of communication between different health professionals. “One would give you hormones, the other one would say, ‘Be careful, you shouldn’t take this one.’” Unable to find an integrated solution, Ouhsine eventually created it herself, recently opening Menovivre.

Her experience is, unfortunately, far from unique. When Donna Howarth, founder and CEO of Middle East Menopause Organisation (MEMO), began experiencing perimenopause in 2020, she didn’t immediately recognise it either, despite working in health and fitness at the time. “Things were changing physically and emotionally, and there were moments when I just didn’t feel like myself. I had symptoms, but I didn’t necessarily connect the dots”, she recalls. This struggle was a key reason behind her decision to launch MEMO, “That was one of the biggest wake-up calls for me. If I was struggling to understand what was happening with the background and access to information that I had, what was happening to other women?” she shares. Six years later, thankfully, the landscape is changing. Howarth points out that more doctors are taking an interest in menopause care, there are more workplace conversations and health events, greater HRT choice and, crucially, women arriving at appointments better informed and asking more questions. But gaps remain. “Women are desperately ready for this conversation,” she says. “Often they have simply been waiting for somebody else to start it.”

And increasingly, that conversation extends beyond just the physical. “A recurring phrase is some version of ‘I don’t feel like myself anymore,’” says menopause coach Sara Beattie. Women may initially seek help for hot flushes, sleeplessness or brain fog, she explains, but underneath can sit something harder to define: a loss of confidence in who they are and how they function. “‘What’s happening to me?’ can very quickly become ‘What’s wrong with me?’ Those are two very different questions.”

For most women, it is compounded by timing. Symptoms can collide with ageing parents, children, relationships, career pressure and changing bodies – what Beattie describes as a “perfect storm”. And for those who have spent decades being the ‘glue’, suddenly feeling untethered and unable to function in quite the same way can destabilise identities built over decades. Ouhsine agrees, “I always say perimenopause and menopause is not only a woman’s issue; it’s a family issue, and it impacts everything.” Beattie believes it’s also important that the conversation moves beyond simply managing symptoms. “The goal doesn’t always have to be getting back to the woman you were before menopause,” she says. “There can be an opportunity to decide deliberately what you want to carry forward, what you’re ready to put down, and what comes next.” And perhaps this is where the real change is. Women are no longer afraid to speak about what may make others uncomfortable; instead, they’re taking charge, demanding answers and voicing feelings. After all, nobody prepared us for this. But maybe now is the time we started preparing each other.
