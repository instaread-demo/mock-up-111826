The Smithsonian’s Flying Machine That Plunged Into the Potomac Off the Arsenal in 1903

Charles Manly went into the icy Potomac under Langley’s wrecked flying machine, tore off his cork jacket to get free and was hauled out “blue to the lips.”

On the afternoon of Dec. 8, 1903, work stopped along the South Washington waterfront, because a houseboat with a giant flying machine rigged on its roof was heading down the river.

It anchored on the flats off Arsenal Point, where the Anacostia meets the Potomac, just below Washington Barracks. Crowds lined the Arsenal sea wall, and a fleet of launches, sailboats and rowboats gathered around the spot.

At 4:45 p.m., with darkness coming on and ice floating in the river, a catapult shot the machine off the roof with Charles M. Manly at the controls. It folded up, reared straight up on end and fell backward into the river.

The machine was the work of Samuel P. Langley, the Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, and the War Department had put $50,000 into it. Nine days later, the Wright brothers flew at Kitty Hawk.

So how did the head of the Smithsonian end up dropping his chief assistant into the icy Potomac off what’s now Fort McNair? The Washington Post, for one, had a field day with it.

The Smithsonian Secretary’s Flying Machine

Langley was an astronomer, and in 1887 he left the Allegheny Observatory to become Secretary of the Smithsonian. When he died, the Washington Post said building a machine that would fly had been his “life study.”

Early in 1898, a board of Army and Navy officers looked over his models to report whether a large machine could be built and be of practical value. The War Department’s Board of Ordnance and Fortification then put up $25,000 on Nov. 9, 1898, and another $25,000 on Dec. 18, 1899.

This wasn’t a hobby project. In a statement to the press on Aug. 19, 1903, Langley said the experiments were aimed at a machine for war. The money came from the War Department board, private sources and a special Smithsonian endowment.

His chief assistant since June 1898 was Charles M. Manly, and Manly was the one who would ride the thing.

According to Manly, the War Department money had run out nearly two years before the December launch, and the Smithsonian covered the rest.

Langley also kept reporters at arm’s length. It was the practice of all prudent men, he wrote, “not to make public the results of their work till these are certain.”

The Buzzard and the Ark

The Post had pet names for both Langley’s machine and the houseboat that carried it: the buzzard and the ark.

As Langley described it, the finished aerodrome was built of steel and weighed “complete” about 730 pounds, with 1,040 square feet of wing and two propellers. Its gas engine put out more than 50 horsepower and weighed just 120 pounds.

Manly, who weighed 125 pounds by his own count, put the flying weight at 830 pounds with himself aboard. Langley’s figure doesn’t say whether it counts a pilot, and the two numbers don’t add up neatly, so take each as its author’s.

The Post called it a “sixty-foot aeroplane” without saying what it measured. The Smithsonian today lists its wingspan at 48 feet 5 inches and its length at 52 feet 5 inches.

Langley didn’t plan to take off from the ground. According to Manly, the ark’s roof carried a turntable 48 feet square and a catapult track 80 feet long.

Maj. M. M. Macomb, who watched the trials for the War Department board, called the machine “delicate and frail.”

Into the River at Widewater

Langley’s crew didn’t try it in Washington first. Manly wrote that on July 14, 1903, the houseboat was towed to a mooring in the middle of the Potomac opposite Widewater, Va., about 40 miles below the city.

The first try with a man aboard came on Oct. 7, 1903, and the Post’s dispatch from Widewater described Manly’s flying outfit.

Shortly after 12 o’clock Mr. Manly, wearing a pair of light trousers, canvas shoes, a life preserver, and a pair of automobile glasses, climbed into the car.

A helper fired a skyrocket to warn the photographers on the Virginia shore, and just after noon the catapult let go. According to Macomb’s report, the front guy-post caught on the launching car and dragged the nose down, and the machine plunged into the river.

The Post put the splash thirty yards from the houseboat, and Macomb said about 50 yards.

Manly wrote later that he pulled himself free underwater, swam over to the floating wreck and sat on it until a rowboat came.

Langley wasn’t even there. He said he had been “detained in Washington by business.”

The next morning the Post’s front page read “BUZZARD A WRECK.” The deck below it called the machine “The Costly Contrivance Utterly Unable to Take Wing.”

A storm then wrecked the expedition’s rowboats and launch, according to Manly, and on Oct. 12, 1903, the houseboat was towed back to Washington.

Off Arsenal Point, Dec. 8, 1903

For the second try, the team picked a spot much closer to home, just off the Potomac Flats where the main river meets the Eastern Branch (the Anacostia). River traffic was light that late in the year, Manly wrote.

This time there was no sneaking around. The machine was rigged on the roof before the houseboat even left its slip at the foot of Eighth Street SW.

Getting out there took most of the day. Manly found the tug Bartholdi idle at a wharf, but nobody could find its captain. “The situation demands heroic measures,” Manly said. “Call a carriage and find the tugboat captain.”

It was very late in the afternoon before the boat reached the spot, and by then darkness was coming on and a gusty wind had come up. The river was full of floating ice, blocks several inches thick by Manly’s account.

Along the South Washington river front, a large crowd in a neighboring lumber yard “watched the scientific proceedings with bated breath.” Army officers from Washington Barracks anchored nearby on a government snag tender.

Gen. Wallace F. Randolph, the chief of artillery, was on the houseboat, and so were Dr. F. S. Nash, the photographer Smillie and several of Langley’s guests. Macomb watched from the Bartholdi, about 150 feet off, and Langley was watching too.

At 4:45 p.m. the launching car was released, aimed up the Anacostia toward the Navy Yard. The Post said the nose pointed straight toward “the roofs and towers of the Government Hospital for the Insane,” which is St. Elizabeths.

Not exactly a great omen.

A Crashing, Rending Sound

From the Bartholdi, Macomb saw the rear guy-post seem to drag and bring the rudder down on the track. Then came “a crashing, rending sound” as the rear wings collapsed, though just how it happened, he wrote, was impossible for him to see. The machine reared up, stood on end and toppled backward into the water a few feet in front of the boat.

The Post described it going over.

The enormous construction of steel and wood went completely over, engine uppermost, tail hanging down, and its wings breaking up piece by piece with incredible swiftness. The whole catastrophe occurred in the space of a minute.

To the Post, it looked like “a shapeless mass of stiffly starched sheets.”

Smillie’s cameras couldn’t get a picture in the dark, Manly wrote later, but the Washington Star’s photographer somehow got one. By Manly’s account, it was the one photograph anyone secured of the machine in the air that day.

Back at the Smithsonian, the rest of the staff, taxidermists included, had climbed the tower with telescopes and field glasses. They saw the machine shoot off the houseboat before it disappeared behind a clump of trees, and one of them told the Post, “We thought the experiment had been a success.”

Manly Under the Ice

Manly went under with the machine on top of him. On the houseboat, a Smithsonian mechanic named Fred Hewitt shouted, “He’s tangled up in the wires,” and dove in with his shoes and clothes still on.

The Post’s first story said Hewitt pulled Manly out of the wreck. Manly told the paper a different story the next day. He said he “came up entangled in some of the wires” and had to tear his cork coat off his back to get free. Then, as he told it:

I was clear of the debris when I saw Hewitt poised on the houseboat, in the act of diving. I knew he was coming to my assistance, but by that time I was free of the wreck, and I called to him not to come. He didn’t hear me, but plunged in. It was a brave thing to do, and I certainly appreciate it.

In his 1911 account, Manly added that his head hit a block of ice when he surfaced.

Whoever got him clear, Manly came out “blue to the lips.” Dr. Nash brought him around, and Hewitt was set down next to a red-hot stove inside the houseboat.

That night, on the houseboat, Langley blamed “a defect of the launching apparatus” and praised Manly’s bravery. Manly, for his part, told the Post, “I never felt better in my life.”

The Post Piles On

The houseboat was stuck fast on the flats until flood tide, three hours later, the Post reported. On Dec. 9, 1903, the wreck lay on the houseboat floor at the foot of Eighth Street SW, “a bedraggled mass of twisted steel and splintered wood.”

The Post figured repairs at about $5,000, and Langley guessed less than $2,000. He told the paper he was at a complete loss to explain the accident, yet he was positive the launching gear was to blame.

The Post’s editorial page had heard enough. By its own account it had already poked fun at “the absurd atmosphere of secrecy in which it was enveloped.” On Dec. 10, 1903, it called for the government to get out.

the government should promptly sever its relations with an experiment which has covered eight or ten years of time and involved a very large outlay of the public money without disclosing a single ground for hope.

Nine Days Later

On Dec. 17, 1903, nine days after the Potomac crash, the Wright brothers’ first powered airplane flew at Kitty Hawk, N.C. Orville was piloting, and the flight lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet.

Macomb’s official report on the Potomac trials, dated Jan. 6, 1904, was polite but blunt. The claim of a man-carrying aerodrome, he wrote, “lacks the proof which actual flight alone can give.” Even after a successful test, he warned, years of work and thousands of dollars would still be needed.

On March 3, 1904, the Board of Ordnance and Fortification said it wasn’t prepared to put up more money, at least not “at this time.” In a 1908 report, the board wrote that it had stopped largely because of “adverse opinions expressed in Congress and elsewhere.”

Manly later blamed the newspapers, and he traced their hostility to Langley shutting reporters out of the shops and the houseboat.

Langley died at a hotel in Aiken, S.C., on Feb. 27, 1906, after a second stroke, according to the Post. By coincidence, the paper noted, money for flying machines came up in the House that very day.

The Army’s interest in flying machines didn’t end in the Potomac mud, as the Wright brothers’ trials at Fort Myer would show.

Up the road, Baltimore had its own early brush with flight the day Hubert Latham flew over the city.

Where Langley’s Aerodrome Is Today

According to the Air and Space Museum, the War Department left the wreck with the Smithsonian, and in 1914 the Smithsonian hired Glenn Curtiss to rebuild it. After heavy changes, it made brief straight-line hops at Hammondsport, N.Y.

The museum says the Smithsonian put it on display in 1918 with a label that “misleadingly” called it the world’s first airplane “capable of sustained free flight.” In the museum’s telling, that label helped push Orville Wright to lend his own 1903 Flyer to the Science Museum in London in 1928. The Smithsonian clarified the history in its 1942 annual report.

So what went wrong off Arsenal Point? The record disagrees. Langley, Macomb and the board all blamed the launching gear at the time. The museum today says it was “an overly complex, structurally weak, aerodynamically unsound aircraft.”

The machine itself hangs not in the Air and Space Museum on the Mall but in Pre-1920 Aviation at the Udvar-Hazy Center in Chantilly, Va. (as of 2026).

As for the launch spot, Washington Barracks is Fort Lesley J. McNair today, on the point where the Potomac and Anacostia join. It’s the old Arsenal ground, also the scene of a deadly explosion at the Washington Arsenal.

Langley’s machine was pointed from there up the Anacostia toward the Navy Yard at 4:45 p.m. on Dec. 8, 1903. It made it a few feet, by Macomb’s count.
